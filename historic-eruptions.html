Famous Eruptions

Mt. Vesuvius

Date: 79CE

Trivia: One of the most fascinating facts about the Vesuvius eruption is that it accidentally created one of the world's greatest archaeological time capsules. When ash and pumice buried Pompeii and Herculaneum, they preserved buildings, graffiti, food, household objects, and even the shapes of victims' bodies. In the 19th century, archaeologists discovered they could inject plaster into cavities left by decomposed bodies, creating haunting casts that show people's final moments. The eruption was also so influential that volcanologists use the term "Plinian eruption"—named after eyewitness Pliny the Younger—to describe particularly explosive volcanic eruptions.

Krokatoa

Date: 1883

Trivia: The eruption of Krakatoa produced some of the strangest global effects ever linked to a volcano. The explosion was heard as far away as western Australia and islands in the Indian Ocean, making it one of the loudest sounds in recorded history. Atmospheric pressure waves from the blast circled the Earth multiple times and were detected by instruments around the world. In the months that followed, volcanic dust high in the atmosphere created vivid red and purple sunsets that inspired artists and amazed observers across Europe and North America. Some historians even speculate that these unusual skies may be reflected in certain late-19th-century paintings.

Mt. St. Helens

Date: 1980

Trivia: Mount St. Helens is one of the most studied volcanoes in the world, famous for its dramatic eruption on May 18, 1980. A fascinating piece of trivia is that the eruption was unusual because it began with a massive lateral blast that shot sideways rather than straight up, flattening millions of trees across hundreds of square miles. The explosion reduced the mountain's summit by about 1,300 feet (400 meters), creating the horseshoe-shaped crater visible today. Despite the devastation, scientists have closely monitored the area ever since, and it has become a living laboratory for studying how plants and animals recolonize landscapes after major natural disasters. The volcano is part of the Cascade Range, a chain of volcanoes created by tectonic activity along the Pacific Northwest coast.

Volcanic Regions

Pacific Ring of Fire

The Pacific Ring of Fire is by far the most volcanically active region on the planet. It forms a horseshoe-shaped belt around the Pacific Ocean, stretching through parts of Chile, Peru, Mexico, the western United States, Japan, Philippines, Indonesia, and New Zealand. About 75% of the world's active volcanoes are located here. The region exists because several tectonic plates are colliding and one plate is often being forced beneath another, generating magma.

Indonesian Vollcanic Arc

Although technically part of the Ring of Fire, Indonesia is often highlighted separately because it contains one of the highest concentrations of active volcanoes anywhere on Earth. The country has more than 120 active volcanoes, including famous peaks such as Mount Merapi, Mount Semeru, and Krakatoa. Indonesia sits where several major tectonic plates meet, making eruptions frequent and sometimes highly explosive.

Mid-Atlantic Rim

The Mid-Atlantic Ridge is the world's longest volcanic mountain range, running down the center of the Atlantic Ocean. Most of its volcanic activity occurs underwater, making it less visible than eruptions around the Pacific. Here, tectonic plates are moving apart rather than colliding, allowing magma to rise and create new oceanic crust. One of the few places where the ridge rises above sea level is Iceland, home to active volcanoes such as Eyjafjallajökull and Fagradalsfjall.
